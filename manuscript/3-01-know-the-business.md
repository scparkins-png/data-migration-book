### *The way they work, and the words they use — the thread, pulled together*

## Where You Are

You have finished the seven steps. The data has moved, the counts reconcile, and people are working in the new system. So why is there a chapter *after* Go Live?

Because of the lens you picked up in *Create Your Plan* — *Know the Business — and Its Language* — and have carried through every step since. You could not have written this chapter on day one. Knowing a business — really knowing how it works and the words it uses — is not something you learn up front and then apply. It surfaces as the project moves, right through to go-live. Now you have the whole picture, this chapter helps you pull it together and hand it on.

## Why This Matters

Most of how a business works is not written down. It lives in habits, in workarounds, in one person's head, and in words that mean one thing on the shop floor and another in the accounts office. A migration is one of the few times anyone goes looking for all of it at once.

Underneath every migration, you are learning a business in two ways:

**1. How they work — the process and the unwritten knowledge.** The order things happen in, the exception everyone handles the same way without being told, the "we always do it like this because of that one customer in 2019". This is the knowledge that does not export — and that, if you do not capture it, walks out of the door when someone leaves.

**2. What they call things — the terminology.** Every business speaks three dialects at once: the everyday words its people use, the labels its systems show, and the meaning underneath that ties them together. When those fall out of step, mapping breaks — silently, until it is expensive.

## The living translator

The practical tool for the second kind of knowledge is the **Terminology Translator** you have been keeping since the start — *our word / the system's word / what it really is*. By now it is not a reference table copied from a book. It is a record of how *your* business actually talks, and where its language collides with the new system.

Keep it with the project handover, and give it an owner. It is the cheapest insurance a business will ever buy against knowledge walking out of the door. The full reference — all seven business areas, plus the "false friends" that cause the most trouble — is in **Appendix E**.

## From the Field

### *Sage 50 → Sage X3: the whole argument in miniature*

The assumption at the start of this project — *"same software family, must be simple"* — was not a technology mistake. It was a *business-knowledge* mistake. The difficulty was never the software; it was everything the business knew but had never written down.

Much of that knowledge lived in one or two people's heads. The process that ran the operation had grown up over years and was never documented — it worked because the people running it carried it in memory. More than 1,500 assembly instructions existed nowhere but in those memories, and had to be created from scratch. Product codes had drifted into free text, and the stock records did not match the warehouse. None of that appears in a data export. All of it is *business* knowledge, and it only came to light because the migration went looking.

By the end, that knowledge existed *outside* those heads for the first time: in the master set, the mapping rules, the documented loading order and the process notes. That is the quiet second product of every migration. You set out to move data; you finish having captured how the business actually works. Don't let that understanding evaporate the week after go-live — it is worth more than the software.

## What you have achieved

You have turned a migration into a record of how the business actually works — its processes, its exceptions, and the shared language that keeps its systems talking to each other. The data moved; the knowledge stayed. That is the difference between a business that comes through a system change stronger and one that quietly loses a piece of itself in the move.

## Looking Further

The seven Flow7 steps were built for data migration, but they are not limited to it. The same sequence — sources, quality, master, target, mapping, testing, go-live — applies to any data project: reporting and analytics builds, workflow automation, and AI implementations. The questions at each step change to fit the work; the order and the discipline do not.
