# Foreword

Let me start with the moment a migration usually goes wrong. It isn't the night of the go-live, and it isn't when an import fails. It's months earlier, in an ordinary meeting, when someone says: *"It's fine — the data's all in the system, we'll just move it across."*

I've learned to push back on that sentence, because it's almost never true. The data is rarely "all in the system." A good part of it lives in the head of the person who's worked there longest. Some of it is in the way things are done — an ordering process that grew up over years, that nobody wrote down, that works because everyone just *knows*. And a surprising amount of it is tied up in language: the words a business uses for its own customers, products and processes, which the new system has never heard of and won't accept.

My name is Stuart Parkins, and for more than twenty-five years I've worked with businesses when their data has to move — between CRMs, accounting systems, ERP platforms, eCommerce stores and the web of spreadsheets that quietly holds most companies together. I've both run the migrations and been a part of a team where they went smoothly and, more usefully for you, I've seen the ones that didn't and my experience has saved a few. What I want to hand you in this book is the thing I wish every business had before they started: a targeted way through a data migration that doesn't just depend on being technical, and doesn't end in panic.

Because here is what I've learned, data migrations very rarely fail on the technology. The software almost always works. They fail on the three things that live *around* the data and never quite make it into a clear plan:

- **Your people.** The knowledge that keeps a business running is often trapped in one or two long-serving heads and written down nowhere. On one real project I'll walk you through, the product recipes and assembly instructions existed only in a couple of employees' memories — over 1,500 of them had to be created from scratch before anything could move. If those people had left, that knowledge would have walked out of the door with them.
- **Your business processes.** Data isn't just numbers sitting in fields; it's the by-product of how you actually work — how an order gets raised, how a customer gets set up, the little workarounds the team invented years ago. Move the data without understanding the process that created it and you arrive in the new system with information nobody can use in context.
- **Your terminology.** Every business has its own language. The new system has its own too — mandatory fields, fixed picklists, character limits, its own idea of what a "customer" or a "product" even is. A migration is, in large part, the quiet work of translating one into the other so nothing important is lost in the move.

Making sure those three factors are key, means the technical migration becomes much easier — which, believe me, is exactly what you want it to be. Ignore them, and no import tool on earth will save you. That's actually good news: people, process and language are things you can understand and get in front of. You don't need to be a specialist. You need a method.

That method is the **Flow7 Migration System** — seven ordered steps I developed over those twenty-five years for my own processes that apply to moving data. Flow7 takes the whole daunting job and breaks it down, from finding out where your data really lives, through getting honest about its condition, all the way to going live and confirming it worked. You never have to hold the entire migration in your head at once. You do the step you're on, properly, then move to the next and move back and forth where required.

This is a handbook, not a lecture. Each step-chapter tells you what you're doing and why it matters, shows you the specific trap it helps you avoid, then hands you a checklist and a short exercise to do right on the page. Where the real work is too big for paper — mapping hundreds of fields, testing thousands of records — I point you to tools or approaches built for exactly that job. It is not a technical data book. The thinking happens here; the heavy lifting happens in the proper tools. Work through it and, by the end, you'll have *done* a migration rather than merely read about one. This applies from small one to one system migrations to huge multi business, multi system migrations.

To keep it grounded, one real migration threads through the whole book: a **Sage 50 to Sage X3** move for a manufacturer — the classic "same software family, how hard can it be?" job that turns out to be anything but. You'll see that project step by step and watch where people, process and terminology made all the difference. Alongside it I've woven in other examples from real work I've done. Where a story involves a live client I've changed the details, and in places built composite characters, so the lesson survives without exposing anyone's business. The people may be part-fiction; the problems are entirely real.

A fair warning: you may get kick back across the business, especially from a time and resources angle. The early steps ask you to look honestly at your own data and the way your business really runs, and honest answers can increase both time and resources. You may find sources nobody admitted to, processes nobody documented, and "the one system of record" turning out to be six. That isn't a sign you're doing badly — it's the entire point of doing the work now, on the page, rather than discovering it live in front of your customers.

So take a breath. A migration can feel daunting, is worrying, but is more comfortable when managed systematically. A sequence of manageable steps, done in the right order, with your people and your ways of working brought along rather than left behind will smooth the way. Do that, and the migration panic starts to lift — not because the job got smaller( it may be a huge migration!), but because you finally have a way through it. Let's get your data moving.
