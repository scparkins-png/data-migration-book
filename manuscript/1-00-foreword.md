# Foreword

Let me start with the moment a migration usually goes wrong. It isn't the night of the go-live, and it isn't when an import fails. It's months earlier, in an ordinary meeting, when someone says: *"It's fine — the data's all in the system, we'll just move it across."*

I've learned to push back on that sentence, because it's almost never true. The data is rarely "all in the system". A good part of it lives in the head of the person who's worked there longest. Some of it is in the way things are done — an ordering process that grew up over years, that nobody wrote down, that works because everyone just *knows*. And a surprising amount of it is tied up in language: the words a business uses for its own customers, products and processes, which the new system has never heard of and won't accept.

My name is Stuart Parkins, and for more than twenty-five years I've worked with businesses when their data has to move — between CRMs, accounting systems, ERP platforms, eCommerce stores and the web of spreadsheets that quietly holds most companies together. I've run migrations myself and worked in teams where they went smoothly. More usefully for you, I've seen the ones that didn't — and helped rescue a few. What I want to hand you in this book is the thing I wish every business had before it started: a clear way through a data migration that doesn't depend on being technical, and doesn't end in panic.

Because here is what I've learned: data migrations very rarely fail on the technology. The software almost always works. They fail on the three things that live *around* the data and never quite make it into the plan:

- **Your people.** The knowledge that keeps a business running is often trapped in one or two long-serving heads and written down nowhere. On one real project I'll walk you through, the product recipes and assembly instructions existed only in a couple of employees' memories — over 1,500 of them had to be created from scratch before anything could move. If those people had left, that knowledge would have walked out of the door with them.
- **Your business processes.** Data isn't just numbers sitting in fields; it's the by-product of how you actually work — how an order gets raised, how a customer gets set up, the little workarounds the team invented years ago. Move the data without understanding the process that created it, and you arrive in the new system with information nobody can use.
- **Your terminology.** Every business has its own language. The new system has its own too — mandatory fields, fixed picklists, character limits, its own idea of what a "customer" or a "product" even is. A migration is, in large part, the quiet work of translating one into the other so nothing important is lost in the move.

Get those three right, and the technical migration becomes much easier — which, believe me, is exactly what you want. Ignore them, and no import tool on earth will save you. That's actually good news: people, process and language are things you can understand and get in front of. You don't need to be a specialist. You need a method.

That method is the **Flow7 Migration System** — seven ordered steps I developed over those twenty-five years for moving data. Flow7 breaks the whole daunting job down: from finding out where your data really lives, through getting honest about its condition, all the way to going live and confirming it worked. You never have to hold the entire migration in your head at once. You do the step you're on, properly, then move to the next — and step back when the work tells you to.

This is a handbook, not a lecture. Each step chapter tells you what you're doing and why it matters, shows you the trap it helps you avoid, then gives you a short checklist and an exercise to try on your own data. Where the real work is too big for a page — mapping hundreds of fields, testing thousands of records — I point you to the templates and tools built for exactly that job. The thinking happens here; the heavy lifting happens in the proper tools. Work through it and, by the end, you'll have *done* a migration rather than merely read about one — whether it's a simple move from one system to another or a large multi-business, multi-system programme.

To keep it grounded, one real migration runs through the whole book: a **Sage 50 to Sage X3** move for a manufacturer — the classic "same software family, how hard can it be?" job that turns out to be anything but. You'll follow that project step by step and see where people, process and terminology made all the difference.

A fair warning: you may get push-back across the business, especially about time and resources. The early steps ask you to look honestly at your data and at the way your business really runs, and honest answers often show there is more work than anyone expected. You may find sources nobody admitted to, processes nobody documented, and "the one system of record" turning out to be six. That isn't a sign you're doing badly. It's the entire point of doing the work now, at your own pace, rather than discovering it live in front of your customers.

So take a breath. A migration can feel daunting, but it becomes far calmer when you treat it as a sequence of manageable steps, done in the right order, with your people and your ways of working brought along rather than left behind. Do that, and the panic starts to lift — not because the job got smaller (it may still be a huge migration!), but because you finally have a way through it. Let's get your data moving.
