# Critical Edit — Foreword

27 October 2026 · branch `critical-edit-foreword`

## Verdict

This is the strongest piece of writing in the book, and it needs the lightest touch. The opening scene ("the data's all in the system"), the three failure points (people, process, terminology) and "you don't need to be a specialist; you need a method" do exactly what a foreword should. Nothing structural changes.

What does need fixing:
- **Seven copy-edit items** (C1–C7), mostly long or tangled sentences.
- **"An exercise to do right on the page"** — out of date under the worksheet-pack decision (M9).
- **The examples paragraph** repeats How to Use almost word for word ("composite characters", "the people may be part-fiction"). Readers meet it twice within three pages.
- **"Honest answers can increase both time and resources"** says the right thing a slightly confusing way.

Words: about 1,060 → 990. The cuts are small; the point is to make every sentence read cleanly.

## Proposals

**P1 — Your credentials sentence, untangled** (C1). "I've both run the migrations and been a part of a team where they went smoothly and, more usefully for you, I've seen the ones that didn't and my experience has saved a few" becomes three sentences: *"I've run migrations myself and worked in teams where they went smoothly. More usefully for you, I've seen the ones that didn't — and helped rescue a few."* "A targeted way through" becomes "a clear way through"; "doesn't just depend on being technical" becomes "doesn't depend on being technical".

**P2 — Small fixes:**
- colon after "here is what I've learned" (C2);
- "Making sure those three factors are key, means…" → "Get those three right, and…" (C3);
- "kick back" → "push-back" (C4);
- "smaller( it may be…" → "smaller (it may still be…" (C6).

**P3 — "Daunting, is worrying, but is more comfortable"** (C5) becomes *"A migration can feel daunting, but it becomes far calmer when you treat it as a sequence of manageable steps…"*

**P4 — The handbook paragraph brought up to date** (closes M9). "A checklist and a short exercise to do right on the page" becomes "a short checklist and an exercise to try on your own data". "Too big for paper" becomes "too big for a page". The orphaned last sentence about small and huge migrations is folded into the one before it, with the hyphens fixed (C7).

**P5 — The examples paragraph shortened.** The Sage X3 introduction stays. The sentences about composite characters and "part-fiction" are cut, because How to Use now explains the examples (including the illustrative scenario) a page later.

**P6 — The warning paragraph clarified.** "Honest answers can increase both time and resources" becomes "honest answers often show there is more work than anyone expected". "On the page" becomes "at your own pace".

**P7 — Flow7 sentence tidied.** "Seven ordered steps I developed over those twenty-five years for my own processes that apply to moving data" becomes "seven ordered steps I developed over those twenty-five years for moving data". "Move back and forth where required" becomes "step back when the work tells you to", which echoes How to Use.

## Not changed

- The opening scene and the three bullet points (one phrase trimmed: "in context").
- "Ignore them, and no import tool on earth will save you."
- "Let's get your data moving." and the sign-off.

## Question for you — the Introduction (edit log M1)

The plan had a separate Introduction between the Foreword and How to Use, carrying Forbes/McKinsey statistics on migration overruns. Having edited the Foreword, I'd suggest **no separate Introduction**:
- the Foreword already does an introduction's job (why migrations fail, what the method is, what the book is);
- How to Use explains the structure;
- Part One is already a long run-up before the reader does anything.

If you want the statistics, I'd add **one short paragraph to the Foreword**, after "data migrations very rarely fail on the technology" — but only once I've traced each figure to its original report. Many overrun statistics quoted online are second-hand or misattributed, and a wrong one in the opening pages would undermine the book.

Three options:
1. No Introduction, no statistics.
2. No Introduction; I research and verify the statistics and add one paragraph to the Foreword.
3. Keep a one-page Introduction (I draft it, with verified statistics).

Whichever you choose, I'll update *About the Sources*, which currently mentions "the Introduction".
